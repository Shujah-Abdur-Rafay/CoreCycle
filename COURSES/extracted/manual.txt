Welcome to Recycle Right Ontario
Ontario generates over 12 million tonnes of waste every year. Only about a third of that is diverted from landfill through recycling, composting, and reuse. The rest is buried — and landfill space across southern Ontario is running critically short.
But here's the remarkable thing: most of what ends up in Ontario's landfills doesn't need to be there. It can be recycled. It can be composted. It can be dropped off at one of more than 20,000 locations across the province. The problem isn't that Ontario doesn't have the programs — it's that we, as residents, don't always know how to use them correctly.
This training program changes that. By the time you complete all 16 modules, you will have the knowledge to recycle correctly, sort materials at home, safely dispose of hazardous items, understand what happens to your recyclables after collection, and inspire your family and community to do better.

MODULE 2
Know Your Materials

Learning Objectives
Identify the six major material categories in Ontario's recycling system
Understand the correct disposal pathway for each material type
Explain why each category must be handled differently

The Six Material Categories
Ontario's recycling programs handle six major categories of material. Getting them sorted correctly is the foundation of everything else in this training.

1. Metals
Metals are among the most valuable and most recyclable materials on earth. They include aluminum cans, steel food tins, clean aluminum foil trays, and empty aerosol cans. Metals are genuinely infinitely recyclable — they can be melted down and re-formed over and over again without any loss of quality.
Energy savings:  Recycling aluminum saves 95% of the energy required to produce the same amount of aluminum from raw ore (bauxite). A recycled aluminum can is back on a store shelf as a brand new can within 60 days of being dropped in the recycling bin.
Steel recycling:  Over 88% of steel cans are recycled in many leading municipalities. Steel maintains its strength through the recycling process and is used in new food containers, construction materials, and vehicles.

⚠ Important:  Do not place scrap metal, metal pipes, wire, springs, or large metal objects in the blue bin. These damage sorting machinery. Metal appliances (fridges, stoves) are handled through separate municipal large-item collection programs.

2. Paper & Cardboard
Paper and cardboard make up a significant portion of Ontario's blue bin material. When clean, they are highly recyclable — paper fibre can be recycled up to 7 times before the fibres become too short to be useful. Recycling paper saves trees, reduces water use in paper mills, and prevents methane production in landfills.
Accepted: newspapers, flattened cardboard boxes, magazines, greeting cards, calendars, notebooks, envelopes (with or without windows), office paper, promotional flyers, paper bags, soft-cover books.
Under the new Blue Box 2026 program, greeting cards, calendars, and promotional material are also accepted. Note: hard-cover books are not included.

⚠ Important:  Greasy or food-soiled paper CANNOT go in the blue bin. It contaminates paper bales and makes them unsellable. A greasy pizza box, paper plates with food, or paper towels with food residue all belong in the green bin or garbage — not the blue bin.

3. Plastics
Not all plastics are created equal, and not all plastics are recyclable in Ontario. The type of plastic is identified by a number (1–7) inside the recycling triangle symbol on the bottom of containers. The most broadly accepted plastics are #1 (PET) and #2 (HDPE). See Module 4 for the full Plastics Guide.

⚠ Important:  Black plastic of any number is NOT recyclable in Ontario. The carbon-black pigment used to make plastic black absorbs all infrared light, making it completely invisible to the Near-Infrared (NIR) optical sorting scanners used at all Ontario Material Recovery Facilities. Black plastic always goes in the garbage.

4. Batteries
Batteries power our modern lives — clocks, remotes, phones, laptops, power tools, and more. But batteries are one of the most hazardous items that Ontarians routinely misplace in the wrong bin. Batteries contain heavy metals including lead, cadmium, lithium, and mercury — all of which contaminate soil and groundwater when they end up in landfill.

🔴 Safety Alert:  NEVER place batteries of any type in the blue bin, green bin, or garbage. Lithium-ion batteries (from phones, laptops, e-bikes, and power tools) are particularly dangerous — when crushed in collection trucks or MRF machinery, they can ignite and cause fires that have injured Ontario workers and destroyed millions of dollars in equipment.
Accepted for recycling at designated drop-off locations: button cells, AA, AAA, C, D, 9V, lantern batteries, sealed lead-acid batteries, and replacement batteries for devices (phones, laptops, drills) up to 5 kg each. The battery must be separated from the device before drop-off.

📋 RPRA Official:  Find your nearest free battery drop-off at rpra.ca/where-to-recycle. Canadian Tire, Home Depot, Best Buy, and most Ontario municipalities accept all battery types for free.

5. Household Hazardous Waste (HHW)
Many common household products — paints, solvents, pesticides, motor oil, antifreeze, pool chemicals, and fertilizers — are classified as Household Hazardous Waste. They can ignite, corrode, or poison, and must NEVER go in any regular bin. RPRA oversees a province-wide HHW drop-off program with collection at depots and events across Ontario.
Critical fact: Even the EMPTY CONTAINER of a hazardous product is classified as HHW and must go to a depot. For example, an empty antifreeze jug or an empty paint tin should not go in the blue bin — it goes to the HHW depot.

6. Organics
Food scraps, coffee grounds, tea bags, meat, fish, bones, dairy, and soiled paper all belong in the green bin — not the blue bin. Organics processed through green bin programs become compost that enriches Ontario's farmland. Organics sent to landfill instead produce methane — a greenhouse gas 80 times more potent than CO2 over 20 years.

🔴 Safety Alert:  Organics in the blue bin are Ontario's single most common cause of rejected recycling loads. A single container of leftover yogurt or food residue can contaminate an entire truckload of otherwise perfectly good recyclables, sending them to landfill.


Quick Check:  Before this module's quiz, ask yourself: Which material category must NEVER go in any regular bin? Answer: Batteries and Household Hazardous Waste. Both require dedicated drop-off at designated locations across Ontario.

MODULE 3
The Blue Box Revolution — January 1, 2026

Learning Objectives
Explain the Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) model and how it changes Ontario's Blue Box Program
Understand RPRA's role as Ontario's independent recycling regulator
Identify the three expanded material categories now accepted in the new Blue Box
Explain why harmonisation across Ontario matters for every resident

The Biggest Change in Ontario Recycling History
Starting January 1, 2026, Ontario's Blue Box Program undergoes its most significant transformation ever. Under the new system, the businesses that produce or sell products and packaging in Ontario become 100% financially responsible for collecting and recycling those materials after consumers are finished with them.
This approach is called Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR). It means that the cost of the Blue Box Program is no longer paid by Ontario taxpayers or municipalities — it is paid entirely by producers. If a company makes a plastic water bottle, a cardboard cereal box, or a single-use straw, that company is now legally accountable for what happens to it after you throw it away.

What is RPRA?
The Resource Productivity and Recovery Authority (RPRA) is the Ontario government's independent regulatory authority. RPRA was established to enforce the province's recycling laws and ensure producers actually collect and recycle the materials they're responsible for. RPRA currently oversees six recycling programs:
The Blue Box Program (household packaging and paper)
Batteries — all types, all sizes
Electronics — computers, phones, TVs, and more
Household Hazardous Waste (HHW) — paints, chemicals, pesticides
Lighting — all bulb types including LED, CFL, halogen
Tires — all vehicle and equipment tires

📋 RPRA Official:  RPRA is not a government department. It is an independent regulatory authority that enforces compliance, sets legally binding recovery targets, and maintains the province-wide Where to Recycle map with 20,000+ locations. Visit rpra.ca for more information.

Harmonisation — Same Rules, Everywhere in Ontario
One of the most important changes under the new Blue Box program is harmonisation. For the first time in Ontario's history, the same materials will be accepted for recycling in every community across the province — from Toronto to Thunder Bay, from Windsor to Kenora.
Previously, what you could recycle depended entirely on where you lived. A plastic container accepted in Ottawa might be refused in Hamilton. This inconsistency confused residents and reduced recycling rates across the province. Harmonisation ends that confusion.

Who is Now Included?
The new Blue Box program also dramatically expands coverage beyond single-family homes. The following are now included for the first time or with greater access:
Apartment buildings and condominiums (multi-unit residential buildings)
Schools and educational institutions
Retirement homes and assisted living facilities
Long-term care homes and nursing facilities

What Three Categories Does the New Blue Box Accept?
The new Blue Box program organises accepted materials into three distinct categories:

Category
What's Included
1. Product Packaging
Primary packaging (the packaging a product comes in), transportation packaging (boxes and bags used by couriers and delivery), convenience packaging (bags from grocery checkout, single-use containers), service accessories (straws, single-use cutlery, plates), and ancillary packaging elements like pouring spouts on juice cartons
2. Paper Products
Newspapers, magazines, greeting cards, calendars (promotional or purchased), notebooks and daily planners, promotional materials, directories and catalogues, and all general-purpose copy or writing paper. Note: hard-cover books and hardcover periodicals are NOT included
3. Packaging-Like Products
Aluminum foil, metal food trays, plastic film and wrap, wrapping paper, paper bags, beverage cups, plastic bags, cardboard boxes, and envelopes of all types. Note: flexible plastic food containment products like cling wrap, sandwich bags, and freezer bags are NOT included in this category


Key Takeaway:  The new Blue Box program starting January 2026 means producers pay, more Ontarians are covered, and the same materials are accepted everywhere. This is the system working the way it should — making businesses accountable for the full lifecycle of what they sell.

MODULE 4
The Plastics Guide — Know Your Number

Learning Objectives

Know which plastic types are accepted in Ontario's blue bin and which are not
Understand why black plastic and plastic bags require special handling

How to Find the Plastic Number
Every plastic container manufactured for sale has a resin identification code — a number from 1 to 7 stamped inside a triangle symbol (the Möbius loop). It is usually found on the bottom of the container, moulded directly into the plastic. This number tells you exactly what type of plastic the item is made from.

⚠ Important:  The recycling triangle symbol on packaging does NOT guarantee that the item is recyclable in Ontario. The symbol may indicate recycled content used in manufacturing the product, or it may apply only to recycling programs in other regions or countries. Always verify with your municipality's accepted materials list.

#3

✗  GARBAGE — Not recyclable

The Black Plastic Warning
Black plastic is made using carbon-black pigment — a substance that absorbs all infrared light. Ontario's Material Recovery Facilities use Near-Infrared (NIR) optical sorting sensors to identify plastic types. Because black plastic absorbs all NIR light, the sensors cannot detect it at all. To the scanner, black plastic is invisible — it appears as if nothing is there.
As a result, black plastic of any resin number — #1, #2, #5, or any other — cannot be sorted at an Ontario MRF and must always go in the garbage. This includes black plastic meat trays, black takeout containers, black plant pots, and black plastic components of any product.

The Plastic Bag Rule
Plastic bags — including grocery bags, dry-cleaning bags, produce bags, bread bags, and zip-lock bags — must NEVER go in the blue bin. They tangle around the rotating disc screens and conveyor belts inside sorting facilities, causing machinery jams that shut down the entire sorting line. A single jam can take a team of workers hours to clear.
Instead, clean plastic bags should be collected together and brought to the grocery store collection bins available at most major Ontario retailers including Loblaws, No Frills, and Walmart. These bags are then sent to specialised facilities that can process them.

📋 RPRA Official:  Check your municipality's specific guidelines for plastics #4, #5, and #7, as acceptance varies across Ontario. From January 1, 2026, the Blue Box harmonisation will standardise plastic acceptance province-wide, reducing this confusion.

MODULE 5
Environmental Impact — Why Recycling Matters

Learning Objectives
Understand the environmental consequences of sending recyclable materials to landfill
Recognize Ontario's recycling performance compared to world leaders

Ontario by the Numbers
Ontario's recycling system has achieved a great deal — but significant work remains. From 2002 to 2021, over 17.6 million tonnes of Blue Box materials were recovered and sold as raw materials for new manufacturing. By 2021, 96.4% of Ontario households were served by the Blue Box program. These are real achievements.
But compare Ontario's overall diversion rate to the world leaders: Sweden achieves an 88.4% return rate for cans and bottles alone. Japan's overall recycling and reuse rate — including industrial materials — reaches 87.8%. Ontario's overall household waste diversion rate sits around 64%. The gap is real, and it represents millions of tonnes of material that could be recovered but isn't.

What Happens When Materials Go to Landfill

Plastics — Microplastics and Wildlife Harm
When plastics end up in landfill or escape into the environment through littering, they do not simply disappear. They gradually break down into smaller and smaller particles called microplastics. These particles — smaller than 5mm — have now been detected in Lake Ontario, in Ontario's rivers and streams, in fish consumed by humans, in agricultural soil, in drinking water, and in human blood samples.
Large plastic items cause direct harm to wildlife — plastic bags suffocate birds and marine animals, and foam pellets from Styrofoam breakdown are eaten by birds and fish who mistake them for food. Single-use plastics are among the most environmentally damaging materials in Ontario's waste stream.

Metals — Mining Pressure and Carbon Emissions
When metals are landfilled rather than recycled, manufacturers must extract new ore from the earth to replace them. Mining is one of the most land-intensive and carbon-emitting industries in the world. It strips forests and topsoil, uses enormous quantities of water and energy, and releases significant greenhouse gas emissions.
Ontario's boreal forest is home to vast mineral deposits. Recycling the metals from Ontario's blue bins directly reduces the pressure on these ecosystems by replacing the need for new extraction. Every tonne of aluminum recycled is a tonne of bauxite ore that doesn't need to be mined.

Paper — Landfill Methane Production
When paper decomposes in a landfill, it does so in an anaerobic environment — without oxygen. This anaerobic decomposition produces methane gas. Methane is a greenhouse gas that is 80 times more potent than carbon dioxide over a 20-year timeframe. Landfills are one of the largest sources of anthropogenic methane emissions in Ontario.
When paper is recycled instead, its fibre is recovered and used to make new paper products. No methane is produced. The trees that supplied the original fibre are also not felled to replace it — saving approximately 17 trees for every tonne of paper successfully recycled.

Batteries — Toxic Metal Leaching
Batteries placed in landfill do not simply sit harmlessly. Over time, their outer casings corrode and the toxic heavy metals inside — lead, cadmium, mercury, and lithium — leach into the surrounding soil and groundwater. These metals are persistent environmental pollutants that accumulate in the food chain.
Lead and mercury from improperly disposed batteries have been detected in groundwater near older Ontario landfill sites. These contaminants are extremely difficult and expensive to clean up once they enter a water system. A single lithium battery also carries an ignition risk — lithium reacts with water, and crushed batteries in collection vehicles have caused dozens of fires across Ontario.

Organics — Methane and Wasted Resources
Food waste is Ontario's single largest contributor to landfill methane. When food scraps, meat, dairy, and soiled paper go into a landfill rather than a green bin, they decompose anaerobically and release large quantities of methane. Estimates suggest that food waste accounts for approximately 40% of all landfill gas generated in Ontario.
The alternative is profoundly positive: food scraps processed through Ontario's green bin programs become high-quality compost that is sold to Ontario farms and landscapers. This compost enriches agricultural soil, reduces the need for synthetic fertilizers, and sequesters carbon from the atmosphere — turning a major environmental problem into a genuine resource.


The Recycling Loop:  When you recycle correctly, your materials travel a remarkable journey: Collected at the curb > Sorted at a Material Recovery Facility > Baled and sold > Remanufactured into new products > Back on the store shelf. That aluminum can you recycled today could be a new can of soup within 60 days. That newspaper becomes tissue paper or packaging. The loop is real — and it starts with you.

Understanding Contamination
Contamination happens when the wrong materials are placed in a recycling bin, or when right materials are placed in the bin incorrectly (for example, with food residue remaining, or inside a plastic bag). Contamination is the single biggest challenge facing Ontario's recycling system.
A contaminated load at a Material Recovery Facility can cause the entire truckload to be rejected and sent to landfill — even if most of the material in the truck was correctly sorted. One greasy pizza box tossed into a blue bin full of clean recyclables can ruin the quality of an entire paper bale.

Common Mistake
Why It's a Problem
Plastic bags in the blue bin
Jam sorting machinery, shut down sorting lines, and contaminate other materials. Take bags to grocery store collection bins instead.
Food residue on containers
Contaminates paper bales and other clean materials. Always rinse containers before placing them in the blue bin.
Organics in the blue bin
Attract pests, create odours, and contaminate paper products. Food scraps belong in the green bin only.
Batteries in the blue bin
Cause fires in collection trucks and sorting facilities when crushed. Take all batteries to designated drop-off locations.
Black plastic in the blue bin
Cannot be detected by NIR sorting sensors. Gets mixed in with other plastics, contaminating bales. Always goes in garbage.
Greasy cardboard in the blue bin
Grease cannot be removed during paper processing. Soiled cardboard contaminates paper bales. Soiled portions belong in the green bin.


The Golden Rule:  When in doubt — rinse it out, or leave it out. It is always better to put a questionable item in the garbage than to contaminate a blue bin load. A correctly sorted garbage item causes much less damage than a contaminated recycling load.

MODULE 7
Hazardous & Special Materials

Learning Objectives
Identify all six categories of household hazardous waste accepted through RPRA programs
Know the correct drop-off method and location for each hazardous material type
Explain why hazardous materials must never go in regular bins
Find the nearest drop-off location using the RPRA Where to Recycle tool

Some household materials are too dangerous, too complex, or too chemically active to be safely handled through regular blue bin, green bin, or garbage collection. These materials require dedicated drop-off programs — and RPRA oversees all of them. There are over 20,000 designated drop-off locations across Ontario, all free to use.

📋 RPRA Official:  Find your nearest free drop-off location for any hazardous material at: rpra.ca/where-to-recycle

1. Batteries — ALL Types
All single-use and rechargeable batteries up to 5 kg each are accepted for recycling at designated locations across Ontario. This includes:
Button cells (watch batteries, hearing aid batteries)
AA, AAA, C, D, and 9V household batteries
Lantern batteries
Sealed lead-acid batteries
Replacement batteries for devices — phones, laptops, power tools (up to 5 kg each)

🔴 Safety Alert:  Lithium-ion batteries (from phones, laptops, e-bikes, scooters, and power tools) are a leading cause of fires in Ontario collection trucks and Material Recovery Facilities. When crushed, they can ignite instantly. ALWAYS remove batteries from devices and take them to a drop-off location. Never put them in any bin or bag with other waste.
Note: Batteries must be separated from the device before drop-off. You cannot drop off an old phone with the battery still inside — remove the battery first, then take both to the appropriate drop-off locations (phone to electronics drop-off, battery to battery drop-off).

2. Paints, Coatings & Solvents
Paint is one of the most common household hazardous waste materials in Ontario. This includes: latex and oil-based paints, aerosol spray paints, wood stains and varnishes, primers and sealers, paint thinners and strippers, acetone, and other solvents such as 3-in-1 multipurpose oil.
PaintCare Ontario accepts leftover paint for free at hundreds of participating retailers across the province. Never pour paint down the drain or into the garbage — it contaminates waterways and is classified as hazardous waste.

⚠ Important:  Critically: Even the EMPTY CONTAINER of a paint or solvent product is classified as Household Hazardous Waste. An empty paint tin, empty solvent bottle, or empty aerosol paint can should NOT go in the blue bin — it must be taken to an HHW depot.

3. Motor Oil, Antifreeze & Filters
Used motor oil, oil filters, antifreeze, and other automotive fluids must be taken to designated collection sites. Canadian Tire and Napa Auto Parts accept used motor oil and oil filters for free across Ontario. Antifreeze must go to an HHW depot.

🔴 Safety Alert:  A single litre of motor oil can contaminate one million litres of drinking water. Never pour motor oil, antifreeze, or other automotive fluids down a drain, into a storm sewer, or onto the ground. These are persistent environmental contaminants.

4. Pesticides, Herbicides & Fertilizers
Fungicides, herbicides, insecticides, and fertilizers must be taken to municipal hazardous waste depots during collection events, or to participating retailers. Check ReturnToRetailer.ca for locations that accept specific pesticide products year-round.
Never pour pesticides or herbicides onto the ground, into storm drains, or in regular garbage. These chemicals are designed to kill living organisms — when they enter soil and waterways, they cause extensive ecological damage. The packaging is also classified as HHW.

5. Mercury-Containing Devices & Lighting
Mercury is a highly toxic heavy metal that accumulates in the food chain and cannot be removed from water systems. Mercury-containing products that require special disposal include: thermometers, thermostats, barometers, fluorescent light tubes, and compact fluorescent (CFL) bulbs.
Home Depot and IKEA locations across Ontario accept all types of fluorescent and CFL bulbs for free recycling. These should never go in the garbage where they will break and release mercury vapour.
Through RPRA's lighting program, all bulb types are accepted: incandescent, fluorescent, CFL, halogen, LED, high-intensity discharge (HID), and ultraviolet bulbs. Note: lighting fixtures are not accepted — only bulbs and tubes.

6. Pressurized Containers
Non-refillable pressurized containers — including helium cylinders, nitrogen cylinders, and propane canisters — are accepted at HHW depots and participating retailers. Refillable propane containers are also accepted for safe disposal.

🔴 Safety Alert:  Never puncture, crush, incinerate, or put pressurized containers in any bin. They can explode. Always take them to a designated depot. Find your nearest location at rpra.ca/where-to-recycle.

7. Medications
Unused, expired, or unwanted medications should be returned to any Ontario pharmacy for safe disposal. This is a free service available province-wide — no prescription required, no questions asked.
NEVER flush medications down the toilet or put them in the garbage. Medications that enter water systems are extremely difficult to remove and affect aquatic life. Many drugs are detected in Ontario's surface water — flushed medications are a significant contributor.

MODULE 8
Preparing Your Blue Bin — 8 Essential Steps

Learning Objectives
Apply all 8 blue bin preparation steps before placing items in the bin
Explain why each step prevents contamination at the source
Identify the most common blue bin preparation mistakes and how to avoid them

What you do with recyclables BEFORE they go into the blue bin is just as important as what you put in. Good preparation at home dramatically reduces contamination at sorting facilities, saves thousands of hours of manual sorting labour, and means more of your recycled material actually reaches manufacturers.

STEP 1
Empty & Rinse All Containers
Use cold water — no soap or dishwasher is needed. The goal is simply to remove visible food residue from jars, cans, bottles, and tubs. Even a small amount of food left in a container can contaminate an entire bale of recyclable paper at the sorting facility. Shake out excess water after rinsing. A quick 3-second rinse under a cold tap is all that's required.

STEP 2
Remove & Separate Lids
Lids are often made of a different type of plastic than the container itself — for example, a #2 HDPE bottle may have a #5 PP lid. Remove lids and recycle them separately if accepted in your municipality. Metal lids from glass jars (like pickle or jam jars) can go back in the jar, or be placed loose inside a steel food can before putting in the blue bin, to prevent small items from falling through sorting screens.

STEP 3
Flatten All Cardboard
Cardboard boxes left as three-dimensional objects take up enormous space in collection trucks and on sorting conveyor belts — reducing the efficiency of every collection run. Break down all cardboard flat before placing it in the bin. Remove any Styrofoam inserts or plastic packaging from inside boxes (these go in the garbage). Tape on cardboard boxes is acceptable in small amounts.

STEP 4
Never Bag Your Recyclables
This is one of the most common and most damaging mistakes Ontario residents make. Placing recyclables inside a plastic bag and then putting the whole bag in the blue bin means that bag will be treated as garbage at the sorting facility — the machinery cannot open it, and the entire bag and its contents will be sent to landfill. Always place items loose and individually in the blue bin. If you collect small items in a bag to carry them to the bin, empty the bag first.

STEP 5
Contain Shredded Paper
Shredded paper causes significant problems at sorting facilities — the tiny pieces fall through disc screens and mix with glass fragments, contaminating other material streams. Rather than placing shredded paper loose in the blue bin, collect it in a paper bag or envelope, seal it, and then place the sealed envelope or bag in the blue bin. The sealed container will stay together through the sorting process.

STEP 6
Let Items Air Dry
Wet paper is fragile paper — and wet recyclables add unnecessary weight to collection loads. After rinsing containers, let them drain and air dry briefly before placing them in the bin. A wet paper bag or wet cardboard box breaks apart easily during mechanical sorting, contaminating other materials. Wet paper also promotes mould growth in collection vehicles during delays. A quick shake and 5 minutes of air drying is sufficient.

STEP 7
Scrape Out All Food
Containers that held peanut butter, tomato sauce, yogurt, or similar sticky foods should be scraped out with a rubber spatula before rinsing. If a container is too soiled to clean reasonably — for example, if a large amount of hardened food is stuck to the inside — it belongs in the garbage rather than the blue bin. There is no shame in throwing away an unsalvageable container. An unrinsed container that contaminates a bale does more harm than a container in the garbage.

STEP 8
Set Up a Home Sorting Station
The most effective single change you can make in your household is to set up a dedicated sorting station — three clearly labelled containers under your kitchen sink or in your laundry room: one for recycling, one for compost/organics, and one for garbage. Using picture-based labels (especially important for children, seniors, and households where English is a second language) dramatically reduces sorting errors. Make sorting automatic and effortless, and the whole household will do it correctly.


Time Required:  Following all 8 steps adds approximately 10–15 extra seconds per container to your kitchen routine. Multiply that across Ontario's 5.4 million households, and the collective time invested saves Ontario's sorting facilities millions of dollars in contamination handling costs every year.

MODULE 9
Inside the Sorting Facility (MRF)

Learning Objectives
Describe the six stages of the sorting process at an Ontario Material Recovery Facility
Explain what happens to correctly sorted Blue Box materials according to RPRA
Understand how contamination at home creates problems and costs at the MRF
Connect specific home sorting behaviours to their downstream impacts at the facility

Ontario's Material Recovery Facilities — known as MRFs (pronounced "murfs") — are sophisticated industrial operations that process hundreds of tonnes of mixed recyclables every single day. Understanding how they work helps explain why everything you learned in Module 6, 7, and 8 matters so much.

📋 RPRA Official:  RPRA has confirmed that all properly sorted Blue Box materials DO continue through the recycling process and are eventually sold as raw materials for new manufacturing. Only materials that are contaminated — things that do not belong in the Blue Box — are discarded at MRFs. Recycling is not "going to landfill anyway."

The Six Sorting Phases

🚛
Phase 1: Tipping Floor & Initial Pre-Sort
Collection trucks back up to the tipping floor and deposit their loads. Workers do an initial visual inspection and manually remove obvious contaminants — plastic bags, garbage bags, loose batteries, large hazardous items, and anything that clearly does not belong. This is the first and most expensive line of defence against contamination. A highly contaminated load may be identified here and directed entirely to landfill without proceeding through sorting — making source sorting at home critically important.

🔄
Phase 2: Conveyor Belts & Manual Picking
Material travels on slow conveyor belts where teams of human sorters stand at picking stations. Their job is to remove plastic bags, remaining large contaminants, and items that the automated systems cannot handle. A single collection load containing many plastic bags may require workers to stop the line multiple times per shift to clear jams — each stoppage can cost 15 to 30 minutes of lost sorting productivity. This is precisely why plastic bags must never go in the blue bin.

🌀
Phase 3: Disc Screens & Trommel Drums
After initial hand-sorting, material passes over rotating disc screens and cylindrical drums called trommels. These mechanical systems separate materials by size and shape. Flat materials like paper and cardboard pass over the top of the disc screens, while three-dimensional containers (bottles, cans, jars) fall through the gaps. Shredded paper causes serious problems here — it falls through the screens like sand and mixes with glass fragments below, creating a contaminated mixture that is difficult to process.

🧲
Phase 4: Magnetic & Eddy Current Separation
Two types of electromagnetic separation remove metals from the stream. Powerful overhead conveyor magnets (overband magnets) pull steel and tin cans off the conveyor line, dropping them into a steel collection hopper below. Then, eddy current separators — which generate a rapidly changing magnetic field — create a repulsion force that ejects aluminum cans sideways into a separate chute. These systems are highly effective, but they only work correctly when the material stream is clean and the metals are not buried under contamination.

🔬
Phase 5: Near-Infrared (NIR) Optical Sorting
Near-Infrared optical sorters are the most sophisticated technology in a modern MRF. They shine NIR light across the conveyor belt and use sensors to detect how different materials reflect that light. Different plastic resins have unique NIR signatures, allowing the system to identify #1 PET, #2 HDPE, and #5 PP with high accuracy. When a target material is detected, a precisely timed jet of compressed air blasts it off the conveyor and into the correct collection chute. Black plastic absorbs all NIR light — the sensor sees nothing, and the item passes through unsorted.

📦
Phase 6: Quality Control, Baling & Sale
At multiple points in the sorting line, human quality control workers stand at picking stations reviewing material before it reaches the baler. They catch any remaining contaminants missed by automated systems. Clean sorted materials — paper, cardboard, aluminum, steel, PET plastic, HDPE plastic — are then compressed into dense rectangular bales by powerful baling machines. These bales are sold to manufacturers who use them as raw material inputs. The price paid for each bale depends entirely on its purity. A contaminated bale may be rejected by the buyer entirely — and the MRF operator (and ultimately Ontario municipalities and taxpayers) absorbs the financial loss.

What Happens After Baling?
Once bales leave the MRF, they are sold on commodity markets to domestic and international manufacturers. Ontario's recovered aluminum goes to aluminum smelters. Recovered paper goes to paper mills. Recovered plastics go to plastic processors who turn them into pellets for making new products. The entire system only works when the materials in the bales are pure and uncontaminated — which is why everything you do at home matters.

🔴 Safety Alert:  Battery Fire Alert: Lithium-ion batteries placed in blue bins are crushed in collection trucks, causing fires that damage vehicles, injure drivers, and have shut down entire MRF operations for days. Across Ontario, battery-related fires at sorting facilities have cost millions of dollars in equipment damage. Always remove batteries and drop them off at a designated location.

MODULE 11
The Circular Economy — Ontario's Sustainable Future

Learning Objectives
Explain the difference between a linear economy and a circular economy
Understand how Ontario's EPR laws support the transition to a circular economy
Identify your role as a consumer in closing the circular economy loop
Connect Ontario's recycling progress to the broader global circular economy movement

Linear vs. Circular — What's the Difference?
Our current economic model has largely been linear: we extract raw materials from the earth, use them to manufacture products, sell and use those products, and then dispose of them in landfill. This is a one-way, take-make-waste system that is fundamentally unsustainable — it depletes resources, pollutes environments, and generates waste faster than the planet can absorb it.
A circular economy is fundamentally different. In a circular economy, products and packaging are designed from the beginning to generate as little waste as possible. When products reach the end of their useful life, the materials they contain are collected, processed back into raw materials, and reintegrated into the manufacturing of new products. The circle closes — nothing is wasted if the system works correctly.

How Ontario's EPR Laws Drive the Circular Economy
Ontario's Extended Producer Responsibility legislation — particularly the Blue Box transition taking effect January 1, 2026 — is a powerful driver of circular economy principles. By making producers financially responsible for what happens to their packaging after you use it, EPR creates a direct economic incentive for businesses to:
Design packaging that is easier to recycle, reuse, or compost from the outset
Use less packaging material overall, reducing waste generation
Invest in more recyclable materials rather than composite or multi-layer packaging that is difficult to process
Support the development of collection and processing infrastructure
Increase the recycled content in their products and packaging

The Five Links in Ontario's Circular Economy Chain

Stage
What Happens & Why It Matters
1. DESIGN
Products and packaging are designed to minimize waste and maximize recyclability before they are even manufactured. This is where producers' financial responsibility under EPR creates the strongest incentive for change.
2. COLLECT
Materials are collected from consumers through Blue Box programs, green bin programs, drop-off locations, and take-back schemes. This is where resident participation is most critical — correct sorting at home ensures materials actually reach the next stage.
3. PROCESS
Collected materials are sorted at MRFs, cleaned, and prepared as secondary raw materials. Quality at this stage depends entirely on how well materials were sorted at the source. Clean, uncontaminated materials have high market value.
4. REMANUFACTURE
Recovered aluminum, steel, paper fibre, glass, and plastic resin are sold to manufacturers as raw material inputs, replacing the need to extract virgin materials. Ontario's recovered aluminum is processed into new cans within 60 days. Recovered paper becomes new packaging, tissue, and newsprint.
5. BUY RECYCLED
The loop only truly closes when consumers choose products made with recycled content. By seeking out recycled-content products and choosing more recyclable packaging, consumers signal market demand that drives further investment in the circular economy.

Ontario's Progress
From 2002 to 2021, Ontario's Blue Box program recovered over 17.6 million tonnes of materials and sold them as raw materials for manufacturing — a genuine circular economy achievement at scale. By 2021, 96.4% of Ontario households were served by the program.
The 2026 EPR transition sets even more ambitious targets by making producers legally accountable for recovery rates. For the first time, there is a direct financial consequence for producers when Ontario's recycling targets are not met — a powerful alignment of economic incentives with environmental goals.

Your Role:  You are the first and most critical link in Ontario's circular economy chain. Every container you rinse, every correctly sorted material, every battery you drop off, every purchasing choice you make for recyclable packaging — all of it feeds the circular economy and reduces Ontario's dependence on landfills and virgin material extraction.

MODULE 12
Recycling Myths — Officially Busted by RPRA

Learning Objectives
Identify the four most damaging recycling myths in Ontario
Explain the official RPRA position on each myth with supporting evidence
Counter these myths confidently when you hear them from family, neighbours, or colleagues
Apply correct recycling behaviour based on facts rather than misconceptions

Misinformation about recycling is one of the biggest obstacles to improving Ontario's recycling performance. When people believe their recycling "ends up in landfill anyway" or that "consumers can't really make a difference," they stop trying — and that disengagement has real costs. RPRA has officially addressed the most damaging myths below.

MYTH #1: "Most Blue Box materials end up in landfill anyway — so why bother?"

❌ THE MYTH:  Recycling is pointless because sorting facilities just throw everything in the landfill. The recycling symbol is just a feel-good label that doesn't lead to actual recycling.


📋 RPRA Official:  THE FACT (RPRA Official): Materials are picked up by a collection truck and dropped off at a facility to be properly sorted. During the sorting process, only materials that are contaminated — items that do not belong in the Blue Box — are discarded and sent to landfill. All other materials that are collected through the Blue Box Program continue through the recycling process and are eventually sorted, processed, and sold as raw materials for use in new products. Only contamination — not recycling itself — causes materials to end up in landfill at MRFs.

What this means in practice: When you put the right things in your blue bin correctly (rinsed, unbagged, appropriate materials), those items DO get recycled. The Ontario MRF system is sophisticated and effective when given properly sorted material to work with.

MYTH #2: "Consumers can't really do much beyond using the Blue Box."

❌ THE MYTH:  Individual action is too small to matter. The real problems are corporate and government failures. What I do with my recycling at home makes no real difference to the system.


📋 RPRA Official:  THE FACT (RPRA Official): Consumers can do their part to make sure their recycling opportunities are maximized by using their buying power to purchase products with more sustainable packaging; following instructions provided by their municipality on how to properly clean and sort materials; and making sure items that are recyclable but cannot go in the Blue Box — batteries, lightbulbs, tires, paint — are dropped off at recycling locations.

Consumer behaviour is one of the most powerful levers in the entire recycling system. Source sorting quality — what you put in the bin and how you put it there — directly determines whether the materials at sorting facilities have value or end up as contaminated waste. If every Ontario household reduced contamination in their blue bin by just 10%, it would save Ontario MRFs millions of dollars annually in contamination handling costs.

MYTH #3: "When unsure, it's safer to throw it in the trash."

❌ THE MYTH:  If I don't know where something belongs, putting it in the garbage is the "safe" choice — better safe than sorry. At least it won't contaminate the recycling.


📋 RPRA Official:  THE FACT (RPRA Official): Some materials are not only harmful to the environment if incorrectly sent to landfill — they are harmful to humans. For example, batteries require special handling because they can leak and hurt someone, or they can cause a fire. There are several steps you can take to determine how to recycle something, such as reviewing RPRA's What Can Be Recycled resources and using the Where to Recycle map to find the correct location.

The "when in doubt, throw it out" rule is only valid when the alternative is contaminating a recycling load with food-soiled paper or an unrinsed container. It is NOT valid for batteries, electronics, paint, chemicals, or medications — all of which are more dangerous in the garbage or landfill than at a designated drop-off location.

MYTH #4: "The recycling triangle symbol means it's recyclable in Ontario."

❌ THE MYTH:  If a product has the Möbius loop (the three-arrow triangle) symbol on it, it means the item can and should be recycled in Ontario's blue bin.


📋 RPRA Official:  THE FACT (RPRA Official): The presence of a recycling symbol (known as a Möbius loop) on a product does not guarantee that it can be recycled in Ontario. Refer to RPRA's What Can Be Recycled resources to understand what items can be recycled in Ontario.

The Möbius loop on packaging may indicate that the material is made from recycled content, or that it is recyclable in some regions or countries — but not necessarily in Ontario. For example, a #6 Styrofoam container may carry the recycling symbol, but Styrofoam is not recyclable anywhere in Ontario. Always verify with your municipality's specific accepted materials list or RPRA's Where to Recycle tool before placing an unfamiliar item in the blue bin.

Beyond the blue bin and official programs, there is a powerful set of community-driven innovations that can dramatically accelerate Ontario's recycling outcomes — starting today, in your neighbourhood, without waiting for government or corporate action. The most immediately impactful one starts with something as simple as an aluminum can.

⭐ Community Aluminum Can Collection — The Flagship Idea

Aluminum is one of the most valuable recyclable materials on earth. It can be recycled infinitely without any loss of quality. Recycling aluminum saves 95% of the energy required to mine and process raw ore. And a recycled aluminum can is back on a store shelf as a brand new can within 60 days of being dropped in the collection bin.

At Ontario scrap metal depots and recycling programs, aluminum cans are worth between 5 and 15 cents each. That may not sound like much per can — but community groups, apartment buildings, condo boards, schools, sports teams, faith communities, and neighbourhood associations can collect hundreds or thousands of cans and sell them directly to scrap dealers.
The revenue can fund: additional blue bins and sorting kits for the building, multilingual recycling signage, eco-school certification fees, environmental field trips for students, community park cleanups, or local charity donations.

📋 RPRA Official:  This is exactly the model pioneered by Osaki Town in Japan — a community of 13,000 people that achieved an 83.4% recycling rate for 11 consecutive years and uses the revenue from selling 27 categories of sorted recyclable materials to fund local scholarships. Recycling becomes an economic asset for the community, not just an environmental obligation.

How to Start a Community Aluminum Can Drive
Choose a champion — identify one person or a small group to organize collection in your building, school, or community
Set up a dedicated, clearly labelled collection point — a clean box or bin in a visible, accessible location
Communicate clearly — post multilingual notices explaining what is being collected and why
Set a collection goal — "Let's collect 500 cans this month to fund new recycling bins for our building"
Contact your nearest scrap metal dealer — search "scrap metal Ontario" or "aluminum recycling near me" to find the best price
Celebrate and report back — share how much was collected and what the funds supported, building community pride and participation

Six More Community Innovation Ideas

🏘️
Neighbourhood Can & Bottle Drives  [Community Impact]
Organize monthly or quarterly collection drives in apartment buildings, condominiums, schools, community centres, and faith institutions. Revenue from aluminum can and bottle sales funds additional blue bins, home sorting station kits, or multilingual recycling signage for the building.

🏫
School Recycling Enterprises  [Education]
Ontario schools can run structured aluminum can and mixed recycling collection programs, giving students real-world environmental education while generating revenue for eco-school certification, environmental scholarships, and field trips. Teachers can integrate the revenue tracking into math and social studies curricula.

🤖
Smart Reverse Vending Machines  [Technology]
Inspired by Sweden's Pantamera network, municipalities and retailers can install smart reverse vending machines in grocery stores that accept cans and bottles, automatically sort and compact them, and dispense grocery store loyalty points or transit credits as instant rewards — making recycling as easy and rewarding as using an ATM.

📍
Community Sorting Hubs  [Infrastructure]
Adapted from Japan's neighbourhood waste collection stations, Ontario's high-density urban areas could pilot mini community sorting hubs — small kiosks or collection areas where residents bring glass, plastics sorted by number, and metals separately before collection. Pre-sorting at community level reduces MRF contamination and increases bale purity and value.

🌐
Multilingual Recycling Ambassador Programs  [Inclusion]
Ontario's extraordinary cultural diversity means millions of residents navigate recycling rules in a second or third language. Community ambassador programs — modelled on Japan's self-governing association leaders who personally guide neighbours through sorting rules — can reach every household in their community with trusted, culturally appropriate guidance in 30+ languages.

📱
Gamified Recycling App  [Innovation]
A provincial recycling app — inspired by Japan's smart bin reward systems — could provide address-specific bin guidance for any item, track household and building recycling scores over time, reward correct sorting with real incentives like grocery discounts or transit credits, and make recycling feel like a genuinely engaging daily activity rather than a chore.


Start Today:  You do not need government permission, a big budget, or a formal organization to start any of these initiatives. The community aluminum can collection drive can begin in your building's recycling room tomorrow. The ambassador program can start with a conversation with your neighbour. Every community innovation begins with one motivated person who decides to take the first step.
