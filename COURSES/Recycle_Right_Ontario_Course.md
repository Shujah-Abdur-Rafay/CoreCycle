# Recycle Right Ontario — Public Edition

## Course Description
A complete 16-module training program that equips Ontario residents, students, educators, community leaders, and new Canadians with the knowledge to recycle correctly, sort waste at home, safely dispose of hazardous materials, and contribute to Ontario's circular economy. Built on official RPRA (Resource Productivity and Recovery Authority) data and global best practices from Japan and Sweden, this course prepares learners for the Blue Box 2026 transition and equips them to lead community recycling initiatives.

**Audience:** General public — residents, students, educators, apartment dwellers, community advocates, new Canadians, municipal staff.
**Length:** 16 modules + final quiz.
**Source:** OntRecycle.com Public Training Manual, RPRA, Returpack/Pantamera (Sweden), JCPRA (Japan), UN SDGs.

## MODULE 2 — Know Your Materials

**Description:** The six major material categories in Ontario's recycling system and how each must be handled.

**Learning Objectives**
- Identify the six major material categories.
- Know the correct disposal pathway for each.
- Explain why each category must be handled differently.

**Lesson Content**

1. **Metals** — Aluminum cans, steel food tins, clean foil trays, empty aerosol cans. Infinitely recyclable. Recycling aluminum saves 95% of the energy of mining bauxite. A recycled can is back on shelves within 60 days. *Do NOT* place scrap metal, pipes, wire, or large appliances in the blue bin.

2. **Paper & Cardboard** — Newspapers, flattened cardboard, magazines, greeting cards, calendars, envelopes, paper bags, soft-cover books. Paper fibre can be recycled up to 7 times. *Greasy or food-soiled paper is NOT accepted* — it contaminates entire bales.

3. **Plastics** — Identified by resin code #1–#7 (see Module 4). Plastics #1 (PET) and #2 (HDPE) are broadly accepted. **Black plastic is NEVER recyclable** in Ontario because NIR sorting scanners cannot detect it.

4. **Batteries** — All types contain heavy metals (lead, cadmium, lithium, mercury). **Never** in the blue, green, or grey bin. Lithium-ion batteries cause fires when crushed. Drop off at Canadian Tire, Home Depot, Best Buy, or municipal depots — always free.

5. **Household Hazardous Waste (HHW)** — Paints, solvents, pesticides, motor oil, antifreeze, pool chemicals. Always to an HHW depot. Even *empty* HHW containers are still classified as HHW.

6. **Organics** — Food scraps, coffee grounds, tea bags, meat, dairy, soiled paper. Belong in the green bin only. Organics in the blue bin are Ontario's #1 cause of rejected recycling loads.

## MODULE 3 — The Blue Box Revolution (January 1, 2026)

**Description:** Ontario's Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) transition and the harmonised Blue Box program.

**Learning Objectives**
- Explain the EPR model and how it changes the Blue Box.
- Understand RPRA's role as Ontario's recycling regulator.
- Identify the three new Blue Box material categories.
- Explain why province-wide harmonisation matters.

**Lesson Content**
On **January 1, 2026**, Ontario shifts the Blue Box Program to **Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR)**. The companies that produce or sell packaging now pay 100% of the cost of collecting and recycling it — not taxpayers, not municipalities.

**RPRA** (Resource Productivity and Recovery Authority) is Ontario's independent regulator. It enforces compliance and oversees six programs: Blue Box, Batteries, Electronics, HHW, Lighting, and Tires.

**Harmonisation** — for the first time, the same materials are accepted in every Ontario community, from Toronto to Thunder Bay.

**Expanded coverage** now includes apartments, condos, schools, retirement homes, and long-term care facilities.

**Three accepted categories**
1. **Product Packaging** — primary, transportation, and convenience packaging; service accessories like straws and cutlery.
2. **Paper Products** — newspapers, magazines, greeting cards, calendars, notebooks, promotional material. Hard-cover books are *not* included.
3. **Packaging-Like Products** — aluminum foil, metal trays, plastic film, wrapping paper, paper bags, beverage cups. Cling wrap and freezer bags are *not* included.

## MODULE 5 — Environmental Impact: Why Recycling Matters

**Description:** What happens when recyclables go to landfill, and how Ontario compares to world leaders.

**Learning Objectives**
- Understand the environmental cost of landfill disposal.
- Compare Ontario's diversion rate to Sweden and Japan.
- Explain harm caused by improperly disposed plastics, metals, paper, batteries, and organics.
- Connect everyday actions to measurable outcomes.

**Lesson Content**
Ontario recovered 17.6M tonnes of Blue Box materials between 2002–2021 — a real achievement. Yet our overall household diversion rate (~64%) lags Sweden (88.4% for cans/bottles) and Japan (87.8% overall recycling/reuse).

- **Plastics** — Break down into microplastics now found in Lake Ontario, fish, soil, drinking water, and human blood.
- **Metals** — Mining for replacement ore strips forests and emits huge GHG. Recycling avoids this entirely.
- **Paper** — In landfill, paper produces methane (80× more potent than CO₂). Recycling saves about 17 trees per tonne.
- **Batteries** — Heavy metals (lead, cadmium, mercury, lithium) leach into groundwater; lithium causes fires.
- **Organics** — ~40% of Ontario's landfill methane comes from food waste. The green bin turns waste into farmland compost.

**The Recycling Loop** — Curbside → MRF sort → Bale & sell → Remanufacture → Back on the shelf. Aluminum can to new can: ~60 days.

## MODULE 6 — Sorting at Home: Which Bin?

**Description:** The definitive guide to sorting common household items into the right bin and avoiding contamination.

**Learning Objectives**
- Identify which bin every common item belongs in.
- Recognize Ontario's most common sorting mistakes.
- Explain contamination and why it matters.
- Apply contamination prevention rules at home.

**Lesson Content**

**💙 Blue Bin (Recycling)** — Plastic bottles/jugs/tubs (#1, #2), metal food cans (rinsed), glass bottles & jars, flattened cardboard, clean paper, milk and juice cartons, empty aerosols, greeting cards, paper bags, magazines, clean foil trays.
*Not accepted:* Styrofoam, plastic bags, food-soiled items, batteries, electronics, black plastic, waxed boxes, used tissues, greasy pizza boxes, hardcover books, broken ceramics, HHW.

**💚 Green Bin (Organics)** — Fruit/veg scraps, meat, fish, bones, dairy, eggs/shells, coffee grounds, paper-only tea bags, soiled napkins, soiled pizza boxes, bread, pasta, cooking fats in container.
*Not accepted:* Glass, metal, plastic, plastic bags, pet waste, diapers, liquids poured directly, Styrofoam, clean recyclables.

**🗑️ Grey/Black Bin (Garbage)** — Styrofoam, chip bags, candy wrappers, waxed paper, diapers, black plastic, used tissues, broken ceramics, plastic wrap, frozen-food boxes, rubber/leather.
*Not accepted:* Batteries, electronics, paint, medications, propane tanks, fluorescent bulbs, tires, motor oil — all need designated drop-off.

**Contamination** — wrong items in the bin (or right items prepared wrong) can cause an entire truckload to be rejected to landfill. A single greasy pizza box can ruin a full paper bale.

**Golden Rule:** When in doubt — rinse it out, or leave it out.

## MODULE 8 — Preparing Your Blue Bin: 8 Essential Steps

**Description:** The eight preparation steps that prevent contamination at the source.

**Learning Objectives**
- Apply all 8 preparation steps before binning.
- Explain why each step prevents contamination.
- Recognize and avoid the most common preparation mistakes.

**Lesson Content**

1. **Empty & Rinse All Containers** — A 3-second cold-water rinse removes residue. No soap needed.
2. **Remove & Separate Lids** — Lids are often a different plastic type. Metal lids can go inside a steel can.
3. **Flatten All Cardboard** — Saves space and conveyor capacity. Remove Styrofoam inserts.
4. **Never Bag Your Recyclables** — Bagged items are treated as garbage at the MRF and sent to landfill.
5. **Contain Shredded Paper** — Place in a sealed paper bag or envelope.
6. **Let Items Air Dry** — Wet paper breaks apart and contaminates other materials.
7. **Scrape Out All Food** — Use a spatula. If a container is too soiled to clean, garbage it.
8. **Set Up a Home Sorting Station** — Three labelled bins (blue, green, grey) with picture-based labels.

## MODULE 9 — Inside the Sorting Facility (MRF)

**Description:** A walkthrough of the six phases inside an Ontario Material Recovery Facility.

**Learning Objectives**
- Describe all six MRF sorting phases.
- Explain what RPRA confirms about properly sorted materials.
- Connect home behaviour to downstream MRF impact.

**Lesson Content**

1. **Tipping Floor & Pre-Sort** — Trucks dump loads; workers visually pull obvious contaminants. Highly contaminated loads can be rejected here.
2. **Conveyor Belts & Manual Picking** — Human sorters remove plastic bags and large contaminants. Bag jams cost 15–30 minutes each.
3. **Disc Screens & Trommel Drums** — Flat paper passes over; 3D containers fall through. Shredded paper falls through like sand.
4. **Magnetic & Eddy Current Separation** — Overband magnets pull steel; eddy currents eject aluminum sideways.
5. **Near-Infrared (NIR) Optical Sorting** — Detects plastic resins by light reflection, then air-jets eject them. Black plastic is invisible to NIR.
6. **Quality Control, Baling & Sale** — Final QC, then bales sold to manufacturers. Bale price depends on purity.

**RPRA confirms:** properly sorted Blue Box materials *do* continue through the recycling process. Only contamination causes materials to be discarded.

## MODULE 11 — The Circular Economy

**Description:** How Ontario's recycling fits into the circular economy and your role as a consumer.

**Learning Objectives**
- Differentiate linear vs. circular economy.
- Explain how EPR drives circularity.
- Identify your role in closing the loop.

**Lesson Content**
A **linear economy** takes, makes, uses, and dumps. A **circular economy** designs out waste from the start, then recovers materials at end of life and feeds them back into manufacturing.

Ontario's EPR transition pushes producers to:
- Design for recyclability.
- Use less and better packaging.
- Invest in collection and processing.
- Increase recycled content.

**Five Links in Ontario's Circular Chain:** Design → Collect → Process → Remanufacture → Buy Recycled.

You — the consumer — are the most critical link. Sorting correctly, dropping off batteries, and choosing recycled-content products signals market demand and closes the loop.

## MODULE 12 — Recycling Myths Busted by RPRA

**Description:** The four most damaging recycling myths in Ontario, officially corrected by RPRA.

**Learning Objectives**
- Identify and counter the four damaging myths.
- Explain RPRA's official position with evidence.
- Apply correct behaviour grounded in fact.

**Lesson Content**

**Myth 1:** "Most Blue Box materials end up in landfill anyway."
*Fact:* Only contaminated material is discarded. Properly sorted recyclables continue through processing and are sold as raw materials.

**Myth 2:** "Consumers can't really do much."
*Fact:* Consumer behaviour drives the entire system — buying choices, source sorting, and dropping off batteries/paint/electronics.

**Myth 3:** "When unsure, throw it in the trash."
*Fact:* Only valid for ordinary uncertainties. Batteries, electronics, paint, chemicals, and medications must NEVER go in the trash — they require designated drop-off.

**Myth 4:** "The recycling triangle means it's recyclable in Ontario."
*Fact:* The Möbius loop may indicate recycled content or recyclability elsewhere. Always check your municipal list or rpra.ca.

**Bonus — Eco-fees:** Environmental handling fees are NOT government taxes, NOT set by RPRA, NOT mandatory. Businesses set them at their discretion. If misrepresented, contact 1-800-889-9768.

## MODULE 13 — World Leaders: Japan & Sweden

**Description:** What Japan's MOTTAINAI culture and Sweden's deposit-return system can teach Ontario.

**Learning Objectives**
- Describe Japan's household sorting and MOTTAINAI philosophy.
- Explain Sweden's Pantamera deposit return success.
- Compare both to Ontario.
- Identify lessons Ontario can apply.

**Lesson Content**

**🇯🇵 Japan — MOTTAINAI** ("waste nothing, honour everything")
- 87.8% overall recycling/reuse rate.
- 94% PET bottle recycling rate (vs. 27% in the USA).
- Households sort up to 45 categories in cities like Tokyo.
- A bottle is separated into 3 parts: cap, label film, body.
- 1,000+ waste-to-energy plants with advanced filtration.
- AI sorting robots and smart bins in major cities.
- EPR for packaging since 1997.
- **Osaki Town** — 13,000 residents, 83.4% recycling rate for 11 years; revenue funds local scholarships.

**🇸🇪 Sweden — Pantamera Deposit Return (since 1984)**
- 88.4% can/bottle return rate (target: 90%).
- 3 billion+ containers returned in 2025.
- 180,000 tonnes of CO₂ prevented in 2024.
- Deposit of 2–3 SEK ($0.18–$0.28 CAD).
- Closed loop: cans → cans, PET → PET (no downcycling).
- 45% of returns through reverse vending machines.
- Mandatory minimum 25% recycled content in PET bottles (rising to 30% by 2030).

**Lessons for Ontario** — meticulous sorting, community revenue from recycling, financial incentives, closed-loop quality, and EPR as the foundation.
